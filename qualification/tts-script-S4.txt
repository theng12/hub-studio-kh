The lighthouse keeper's daughter, Elsie, had never once seen the sea in a storm, because her father always sent her to the cellar when the wind began to howl. But on the night of her twelfth birthday, the sky turned the colour of old iron, and the cellar door would not open. She climbed the spiral stairs instead, counting every step, until she reached the lamp room at the top of the tower.

Her father stood at the window, his hands pressed against the cold glass. "Elsie," he said, without turning around. "You should be downstairs."

"The door is stuck," she answered. "And besides, I wanted to see it. Just once."

He was quiet for a long moment. Then he stepped aside, and she looked out at the water. The waves rose like grey mountains and fell apart in a spray of white, and far beyond them, a tiny light flickered and vanished, and flickered again.

"Father, there is a boat out there!"

"I know," he said softly. "I have been watching it for an hour. The light is too weak to reach the rocks, and the lamp is the only thing that can guide her in. But the old mechanism has jammed, and I cannot turn it alone."

Elsie did not hesitate. She ran to the great brass wheel and seized the handle with both hands. "Then we will turn it together," she said. "Ready? One, two, three, pull!"

The wheel groaned, shuddered, and slowly began to move. Her arms burned, and her breath came in short, ragged gasps, but she kept pulling, and so did her father, until the huge lens swung round and a beam of golden light cut straight through the storm. Out on the black water, the little boat turned toward it like a moth toward a candle.

It took an hour before the fishermen finally scrambled up onto the dock, soaked and shivering, and laughing with relief. The oldest of them climbed the tower to shake Elsie's hand. "Young lady," he said, "you saved four lives tonight. What on earth can we give you in return?"

Elsie thought about it carefully, and then she smiled. "Next time there is a storm," she said, "please let me stay upstairs."

Later, when the tea had gone cold and the storm had finally worn itself thin, Elsie sat by the window and watched the first pale stripe of dawn spread across the water. The sea looked almost gentle now, as though it had never wished anyone harm, and she found that she was no longer afraid of it at all.

Her father laughed, a deep, tired, happy laugh that she remembered for the rest of her life. And from that night on, whenever the wind began to howl, the two of them climbed the spiral stairs together, and kept the light burning until the morning came.
